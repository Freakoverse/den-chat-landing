Why DEN Chat?

The platforms you rely on today share the same handful of problems. Different logos, same story: you don't own anything.

01
Edited. Deleted. Without asking.

Platform-wide bans, message deletions, whole communities wiped overnight. Your account, your messages, and years of conversation can be changed or erased on a corporation's terms, with no recourse or appeal.

02
Private? Trust us.

Messages sit in plaintext on corporate servers, readable by staff, bots, and anyone who gains access. Without open-source end-to-end encryption, "private" is a promise you can't verify.

03
Show ID, or you're out.

KYC, age checks, phone numbers, email, sometimes a government ID, handed over just to talk and held against you. Step out of line and you're coerced, locked out, or suspended.

04
They go down, you go down.

It all lives on one company's servers. When they shut down, get acquired, or simply decide you're done, everything and everyone goes with them.

And self-hosting the alternatives doesn't fix it.

The usual escape hatch just moves the pain: a separate account and login for every server you join (ten accounts, ten logins), a UX that breaks across instances. And each one still carries the same four problems above, scoped to whoever runs it.

The fix
One identity you actually own.

DEN Chat was built to solve all of this: keys you hold, encryption anyone can audit, and a network no single company runs.

  • Censorship-resistant
  • Permissionless
  • Encrypted by default
  • No KYC or age verification
  • One account everywhere
  • You own your identity

Going deeper: NIP-CHAT vs. other Nostr community protocols A protocol-level look at how NIP-CHAT (what DEN Chat is built on) compares to NIP-29, Marmot, CORDN and Concord. Tap to expand.

The one idea behind the whole table

These rows are not independent scores. Each protocol is one coherent choice about where authority and addressing live, and every row falls out of that choice:

  • NIP-CHAT: in a public identifier plus a stored key tree (best scaling and discovery, weaker metadata privacy).
  • NIP-29: authority and addressing live in a managed relay (simple, scalable, not private, weak on censorship-resistance).
  • Marmot: in an MLS ratchet (best content secrecy, no pre-join history, heaviest).
  • CORDN: in an MLS group plus a per-group coordinator (Marmot's crypto, but delivery through one self-hostable coordinator per group).
  • Concord: in secret rotating derived keys (strongest metadata privacy, high censorship-resistance, worst removal cost).

Nobody wins. Each is Pareto-optimal for a different priority. Marmot and CORDN share the same MLS core; they differ mainly in transport and trust (Marmot over open relays, CORDN over a per-group coordinator).

The lens this is judged through

Criteria are weighted for a target audience coming off IRC, TeamSpeak or Discord who want out of those platforms' core problems: a central owner that holds, reads or sells their data; deplatforming at the host's whim; a host that can be subpoenaed, hacked or shut down (taking the community with it); surveillance of their own chat; and lock-in with no portability.

It is not judged for a hardcore-privacy-advocate or nation-state threat model. So "good enough to escape a corporate host and its moderation" scores green even if a global traffic-analyst could still learn something. Every protocol here except NIP-29 resists relay censorship to some degree; the differences are of degree, not a property only one design has.

What should a user actually pick?

The answer flips entirely on the lens, so there are three answers.

IRC / TeamSpeak / Discord refugee
NIP-CHAT

For someone escaping a corporate host but not defending against a nation-state, NIP-CHAT clears every core problem (no central owner, E2E content, no trusted server, portable identity) while being the only option that also scales a large public community cheaply (O(log N) removal), supports discovery, and keeps full history. Its one concession, a stable public routing surface, is exactly what this user does not care about.

Snowden-grade / high-threat
Marmot (or CORDN)

Against a powerful, persistent adversary, the deciding properties are forward secrecy and automatic post-compromise security. Only the MLS protocols give both. Marmot for "no privileged component, fully relay-decentralized"; CORDN when you also want the metadata hidden (nothing on public relays), at the cost of a per-group coordinator.

Max metadata privacy, no ratchet needed
Concord

For a small-to-moderate, low-churn community that wants its existence, structure and membership hard for the public to see, and is not defending against device seizure. It is the one option that hides even the community name and channel structure, rotates pseudonymous addresses a relay cannot pin, and trusts no server at all. The cost: expensive removals as it grows, and no public discovery.

In one line: a scalable, discoverable, solidly private, censorship-resistant community goes to NIP-CHAT; a metadata-private, strongly censorship-resistant, low-to-medium-scale community that does not need ratchet-grade secrecy goes to Concord; protect-against-a-state goes to Marmot (or CORDN if metadata-hiding matters most). NIP-29 is never the privacy answer: it reintroduces the trusted central host the whole exercise is trying to escape.

The trade-offs

None of this is free. The freedom, privacy and control come bundled with real responsibility, and we'd rather you know exactly what you're signing up for.

No email, no password

Instead of the familiar sign-up you know, your identity is a cryptographic key. There's a learning curve to holding and managing keys, and it will feel unfamiliar at first.

You hold the keys, so there's no reset

You are responsible for your own keys. There is no "forgot password", no reset, and no support team that can let you back in. Backing them up safely is on you.

Lose your key, lose access

If your key is gone, so is your ability to publish as you and to open your encrypted content. You need to be at peace with that worst case before you rely on it.

Leaked key, leaked everything

A compromised key can expose your data and let an attacker impersonate you, modify, or delete your stuff. The flip side of total control is that the key is the whole castle.

Early, experimental tech

The app is in alpha, an experimental build of a spec that isn't standardized or widely tested yet. Things will break, features may misbehave, and data can be lost. It isn't always as fast or robust as a polished centralized app, and it can use a fair amount of data at times.

Deletion is a request, not a guarantee

Because your data lives across independent servers, a delete is a request those servers should honor, but some may not. Treat anything you publish as hard to fully take back.

Reliable means running your own

The relays and Blossom servers that carry your messages and files are mostly run by volunteers, for free. They can drop old data, go offline, or vanish, and nothing obligates them to keep your stuff. For reliability you can actually count on, for yourself and your friends, family, or community, you (or someone you trust) run your own relay and Blossom server. Free works, but until then, treat posts and files as things that could get lost.

Moderation is in your hands (like internet browsers)

There's no company scrubbing the network for you. What you see is shaped by the relays and hubs you choose, and by your own blocks and mutes. That means you may run into things you'd rather not, and curating your own experience, and reporting anything illegal to the appropriate authorities, is on you, the same way you'd handle it if you came across it in your internet browser.

Voice needs a backend you run

Real-time voice doesn't ride the relay network, so a voice channel needs a media backend you connect yourself. You have two paths: Cloudflare, which has a free tier with a generous monthly allowance that covers most communities, or a LiveKit server you self-host or rent. It's a little setup up front, but it keeps your voice under your control instead of ours, and there's no single server everyone is forced through.

Some will read these as dealbreakers, others as features. Either way they aren't accidents: they're the direct cost of high freedom, real privacy, and individual sovereignty. In one word, the price is responsibility.

Who DEN Chat is for

DEN Chat isn't trying to be for everyone. It's built for a specific kind of person, and it's honest about who it isn't for.

A good fit
It's for you if
  • You want what IRC, TeamSpeak, Discord and the rest offer, without the core problems baked into them.
  • You're tired of walking on eggshells, wondering what gets you muted, banned, or erased next.
  • You want real ownership of your identity, your data, your privacy and your freedom.
  • You're tired of watching the same history repeat, and you want out, or you're already out.
Not a fit
It's not for you if
  • You can't carry the responsibility that comes with being in full control of yourself and your data.
  • You couldn't live with losing or compromising your key, and starting again from near zero with a new one.
  • You expect a company to step in and fix it for you when something goes wrong.

If that's you, that's completely fair, and DEN Chat honestly isn't the right tool for you. You'll be better served by a traditional, company-run app like Discord, Slack or Element, where that control is handed to someone else in exchange for a safety net. Use what fits you, no hard feelings.

DEN isn't chasing the average user or the masses, at least not today, even as it works to make the experience as good as it can be. It's for the people who are done repeating the same story and want a way out.
